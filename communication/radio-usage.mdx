The radio is your lifeline on the road. Every transmission you make either adds clarity to the channel or takes it away — there is no neutral. This guide covers how to format a transmission, when to reach for ten-codes versus plain language, and the discipline habits that keep the channel safe for everyone.

## Making a proper radio call

A well-formed radio call has three parts: who you are, what you need, and a release. Nothing more is required. Anything extra slows the channel down.

<Steps>

<Step title="Identify yourself by callsign">
  Begin every transmission by stating your callsign so dispatch and other units know who is speaking. Do not lead with a greeting, a question, or the word "radio." Just your callsign.

  ```text
  "Sam-7..."
  ```
</Step>

<Step title="State your message clearly">
  Deliver your message in plain, direct terms. Use [ten-codes](/communication/ten-codes) for routine status updates and common requests. Switch to plain language the moment brevity might cause confusion — unusual situations, injuries, and anything that requires the receiver to think twice all warrant plain speech.

  ```text
  "Sam-7, 10-38 on northbound I-215 at Baseline, dark blue Honda Civic, plate Nora-Adam-7-3-1."
  ```
</Step>

<Step title="Release the key">
  When you are done, release the transmit key. Do not sign off with "over and out," "ten-four," or any other filler. If you need a response, end with your callsign or a short question — the silence after key release signals that the channel is clear.

  ```text
  "Sam-7, 10-38 on northbound I-215 at Baseline, dark blue Honda Civic, plate Nora-Adam-7-3-1."
  [release key]
  ```
</Step>

</Steps>

## Radio discipline rules

Consistent discipline makes the difference between a channel that functions under pressure and one that collapses. Every member is expected to follow these rules on every transmission.

<AccordionGroup>

<Accordion title="Listen before you transmit" icon="ear-listen">
  Before you key up, pause and listen for a full second. Confirm the channel is clear. Talking over another unit buries their transmission — and if that unit is calling an emergency, the consequences are serious.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Keep transmissions short" icon="clock">
  Say what needs to be said, then release the key. If you need to pass a long piece of information — an address, a description, a plate — break it into logical pieces and pause briefly between them so dispatch can copy. One long unbroken block is harder to receive than two shorter ones.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Use phonetics for anything spelled" icon="spell-check">
  Names, licence plates, street names, and anything else that gets spelled out go over the air in phonetics. Letters that sound alike — B, D, P, T, V, and C, S, Z — are indistinguishable without them. See the [Phonetic Alphabet](/communication/phonetics) page for both the NATO and APCO tables used in this community.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Emergency traffic takes the channel" icon="triangle-exclamation">
  When any unit transmits emergency traffic or calls a 10-33, every other unit stops transmitting immediately. Hold your non-emergency call until dispatch clears the channel. There are no exceptions to this rule.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Do not use filler or dead air" icon="microphone-slash">
  Avoid saying "um," "uh," "standby," or "how copy." If you are not ready to transmit, do not key up. Dead air on an open key blocks the channel just as effectively as speech.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Correct errors with a short break" icon="rotate-left">
  If you make a mistake mid-transmission, say "correction" and restate the element you got wrong. Do not repeat the entire message. For example: "...plate Nora-Adam-7-3-1 — correction — plate Nora-Adam-7-3-**2**."
</Accordion>

</AccordionGroup>

## Plain language vs. ten-codes

Ten-codes exist to compress routine transmissions, not to replace clear speech in every situation.

| Use ten-codes when... | Use plain language when... |
| --------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Reporting status (in service, en route, on scene) | The situation is unusual or does not map to a code |
| Making routine requests (plate check, driver info) | Someone is injured or in danger |
| Acknowledging routine traffic | Working with an outside agency |
| Calling a standard traffic stop (10-38) | The code would require the receiver to think twice |

<Warning>
  Ten-codes are **not standardized between agencies**. A code that means one thing on this channel may mean something different on a mutual-aid channel. Any time you are working with fire, EMS, or an outside law enforcement agency, drop all codes and speak plainly.
</Warning>

<Tip>
  When in doubt, use plain language. "Shots fired, I need backup at Fifth and G Street" is always faster to process than a code number no one is certain of.
</Tip>

## Example transmissions

The examples below show correctly formatted calls for common situations. Study the structure — callsign first, message next, key released after.

**Clearing a call and returning to service:**
```text
"Sam-7, 10-8."
```

**Initiating a traffic stop:**
```text
"Sam-7, 10-38 on southbound Waterman at Mill, silver Ford F-150, plate King-7-Golf-Nora-3."
```

**Requesting backup:**
```text
"Sam-7, 10-78 — solo unit, combative suspect, northeast corner of Carousel Mall parking structure."
```

**Calling emergency traffic:**
```text
"Sam-7, 10-33 — shots fired, officer down, 200 block of Court Street. Requesting immediate response."
```

**Dispatch assigning a call:**
```text
"Sam-7 and Sam-12, Priority 2, disturbance at 485 W. 5th Street, caller reports a male subject refusing to leave. Respond when available."
```
