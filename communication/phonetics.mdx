Phonetics exist because the radio is an imperfect medium. Background noise, broken transmissions, and letters that sound alike — B, D, P, T, and V; or C, S, and Z — turn an unspelled transmission into a guessing game. By substituting a full word for each letter, you give the receiver two chances to catch it: the word itself, and the letter it represents. Project 909 members draw on both the **NATO/ICAO alphabet** and the **APCO law-enforcement alphabet**, and you should be fluent in both. Dispatch, fellow officers, and fire or EMS units may use either system on any given call.

<Info>
  Only two letters share the same word across both systems: **V (Victor)** and **X (X-ray)**. Every other letter differs, which is the most common source of confusion when officers from different agencies work the same channel.
</Info>

## Numbers

Numerals are transmitted digit by digit — never grouped. The pronunciations below are the standard form; note that three and nine carry modified pronunciations to keep them distinct under poor radio conditions.

| Digit | Spoken as | Pronunciation |
| :---: | --------- | ------------- |
| 0 | Zero | ZEE-roh |
| 1 | One | WUN |
| 2 | Two | TOO |
| 3 | Three | TREE |
| 4 | Four | FOW-er |
| 5 | Five | FIFE |
| 6 | Six | SIX |
| 7 | Seven | SEV-en |
| 8 | Eight | AIT |
| 9 | Nine | NIN-er |

<Warning>
  Zero and the letter O are the most commonly confused pair on air. If there is any doubt, say **"figure zero"** or **"letter Oscar/Ocean"** to make the distinction explicit.
</Warning>

## How to use phonetics correctly

<Steps>

<Step title="Pick one system and commit to it for the whole transmission">
  Do not mix NATO and APCO within a single spelling. Choose one system before you key up and use it from the first letter to the last. Mixing systems creates exactly the confusion phonetics are meant to prevent.
</Step>

<Step title="Say the letter, then the word">
  Transmit as "B as in Bravo" rather than "Bravo" alone. This gives the receiver two chances: the letter catches it if the word is clipped, and the word catches it if the letter is clipped.
</Step>

<Step title="Slow down">
  Phonetics only work when each word is fully spoken and clearly separated from the next. Rushing defeats the purpose. If you are reading a long plate, pause briefly between each character group.
</Step>

<Step title="Spell numbers digit by digit">
  Say "four-two-seven," never "four-twenty-seven." A unit number, address, or plate that gets grouped can be miscopied.
</Step>

<Step title="Read back in the sender's system">
  When repeating information back to confirm, use the same phonetic alphabet the sender used. Any discrepancy between what was sent and what was received will be immediately obvious.
</Step>

</Steps>

## Common points of confusion

<AccordionGroup>

<Accordion title="Alfa and Juliett are spelled that way on purpose" icon="spell-check">
  NATO Alfa drops the **ph** and Juliett carries a **double t** so that speakers of any language will pronounce them correctly — "Alpha" with a hard ph sound and "Julie" without the final t are both common errors. The official spellings exist to prevent exactly those mispronunciations.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Quebec is pronounced keh-BECK, not kwuh-BECK" icon="volume-high">
  The NATO alphabet specifies **keh-BECK** as the correct pronunciation. The anglicized "kwuh-BECK" is a very common habit — if you catch yourself using it, correct it. The distinction matters most when the channel is noisy and every syllable counts.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="APCO wording varies between agencies" icon="triangle-exclamation">
  Some services use **Oboe** for O and **Uncle** for U rather than Ocean and Union. Treat the words on this page as the Project 909 standard. When a caller or outside agency uses a word you do not recognize, confirm the letter before copying — do not assume.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="X-ray is the only non-name entry in both systems" icon="x">
  Every other word in both alphabets is a proper name or a common noun. X-ray stands alone, and it appears in both NATO and APCO. It is spoken as two syllables: **ECKS-ray**. Do not abbreviate it to just "X."
</Accordion>

</AccordionGroup>
