Fire callsigns identify the apparatus, not the person riding it. When you move from one truck to another, your callsign changes with the truck — your name never goes over the air as a callsign. Every transmission begins with the apparatus identifier, and dispatch logs activity against the unit, not against individual members. Understanding the callsign structure is the first step to operating correctly on the radio.

## Callsign Structure

Callsigns are built from an apparatus type prefix and a station number, spoken exactly as written. "E1" is spoken "Engine One," never "E-One" or "Engine First."

| Callsign | Reads As | Unit |
| -------- | -------- | ---- |
| E1 | Engine One | Engine stationed at Station 1 |
| L2 | Ladder Two | Ladder apparatus at Station 2 |
| R1 | Rescue One | Heavy rescue unit at Station 1 |
| T3 | Tanker Three | Water tender stationed at Station 3 |
| M2 | Medic Two | Ambulance unit at Station 2 |
| BC1 | Battalion One | Battalion Chief officer |

## Apparatus Roles

Each apparatus type has a defined primary function. Know what your unit is expected to do before you go en route — the incident commander will assign tasks based on what your apparatus carries.

<Tabs>

<Tab title="Engine">

The engine is the primary fire attack unit and the first apparatus due on structure fires. It carries hose, an onboard water supply, and a pump capable of flowing water from either the tank or a hydrant supply.

The minimum crew for an engine is two members. The officer rides the front right seat and directs the initial attack line. On arrival at a structure fire, the engine crew lays a supply line, pulls an attack line, and begins a primary search unless otherwise directed by command.

Engines are also dispatched on vehicle fires, alarm activations, motor vehicle collisions, and brush fires. On medical calls, the engine may respond as a first responder unit ahead of the medic.

</Tab>

<Tab title="Ladder">

The ladder is an aerial apparatus used for elevated fire attack, roof access, and above-ground rescue operations. It carries the department's main inventory of forcible entry tools, ventilation equipment, and ground ladders.

Request the ladder — or expect it to be dispatched — any time a building is two or more storeys, when roof operations are required, or when a rescue above ground level is reported. The ladder should be positioned for maximum aerial reach before the crew commits to ground-level operations.

Ladder crews are trained in horizontal and vertical ventilation. The ladder officer coordinates with the engine officer on ventilation timing so that the attack crew and the ventilation crew do not work at cross-purposes.

</Tab>

<Tab title="Rescue">

The rescue unit handles vehicle extrication and technical rescue operations. It carries hydraulic rescue tools — spreaders, cutters, and rams — for motor vehicle collisions, as well as rope rescue, confined space, and water rescue equipment depending on the station's assigned specialty.

Dispatch automatically includes the rescue unit on any collision where entrapment is reported. If entrapment is confirmed or suspected on arrival, advise dispatch immediately so that the rescue unit is en route rather than staged.

The rescue crew does not initiate patient care — that is the medic crew's responsibility. The rescue crew removes the patient from the entrapment so that the medic crew can manage them.

</Tab>

<Tab title="Medic">

The medic unit provides patient care and transport on all medical calls and attends fire scenes to cover member rehabilitation and civilian injuries. It is crewed by personnel with at minimum a Basic Life Support certification.

On any call with an active hazard — a working fire, a vehicle leaking fuel, a scene with an active threat — the medic stages a safe distance away and waits to be called in by command. The medic crew does not self-deploy into a hazard area. When the scene is controlled and a patient requires care, command calls the medic in and assigns them a safe access route.

At multi-casualty incidents, the senior medic crew member functions as the Medical Group Supervisor and coordinates triage, treatment, and transport with dispatch.

</Tab>

</Tabs>

## On the Air

Every transmission follows the same three-step pattern. There are no exceptions for short messages or familiar crews — the structure exists so that any member monitoring the channel can follow the exchange.

<Steps>

<Step title="Identify by apparatus">
Open every transmission with your apparatus callsign and the station or unit you are calling. "Dispatch, Engine One." Never use a member's name as a callsign on the fire channel. If you are aboard E1, you are Engine One — not your name, not your rank title alone.
</Step>

<Step title="Mark your status">
Advise dispatch every time your status changes: when you go en route, when you arrive on scene, and when you are clear and available. "Engine One is on scene" and "Engine One is available" are required transmissions, not optional ones. Dispatch tracks all units by reported status — if you do not report it, dispatch does not know it.
</Step>

<Step title="Spell anything ambiguous in phonetics">
Street names, cross streets, names, and any term that could be misheard go out in phonetics. "Alpha-Victor-Echo" is unambiguous; "Ave" over a scratchy radio is not. See the [Phonetic Alphabet](/communication/phonetics) reference page for the full NATO/APCO table.
</Step>

</Steps>
