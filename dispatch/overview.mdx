Dispatch is the single coordination point for every unit operating in the county. Every call for service is received, prioritized, and assigned through dispatch, and every unit on the road reports its status back so that the current picture of available resources is always known. A dispatcher who has lost track of unit statuses cannot send the right unit, cannot start backup in time, and cannot catch a unit that has gone quiet when it should not have. Status awareness is the core of the role — speed and brevity support it, but they do not replace it.

<Info>
If you are new to the dispatch role, read this page first, then work through the [Call Handling](/dispatch/call-handling) guide before you take a live channel.
</Info>

## Call Flow

Every call, regardless of priority or type, moves through the same five stages. Work each stage in order and do not skip ahead — a call assigned before it is fully prioritized is a call that may go to the wrong unit with the wrong response level.

<Steps>

<Step title="Receive">
Take the call and record the essentials before you do anything else: exact location, nature of the complaint, whether anyone is injured, and whether weapons are reported. Incomplete intake leads to incorrect prioritization and under-resourced responses.
</Step>

<Step title="Prioritize">
Assign a priority level based on the threat to life and urgency of the situation using the table below. Prioritize on the information you have now — you can upgrade or downgrade as the call develops.
</Step>

<Step title="Assign">
Broadcast the call to the nearest available and appropriate unit. State the priority first, then the location, then the nature of the call. This order matters: the responding unit needs to know how to travel before it needs to know why.
</Step>

<Step title="Support">
Remain active on the call. Relay updates from additional callers, run plate and name checks on request, and start additional resources — additional units, fire, EMS, or a supervisor — as the situation develops. You do not go passive once a unit is assigned.
</Step>

<Step title="Close">
Once the primary unit clears the scene, log the disposition and return the unit to available status. Confirm all supporting units are also cleared and updated before you close the incident in the log.
</Step>

</Steps>

## Call Priority

Priority is assigned at intake and can change at any point during the call. The priority level determines how a unit travels and how many units are sent.

| Priority | Meaning | Response |
| :------: | ------- | -------- |
| 1 | Threat to life in progress | Immediate emergency response, multiple units dispatched |
| 2 | Urgent, no immediate threat to life | Prompt response, no emergency equipment en route |
| 3 | Routine call for service | Assign when a unit becomes free |
| 4 | Report only, no attendance required | Handled by phone or directed to online report |

<Warning>
Priority is based on the information available at the time and can change mid-call. If a caller reports a weapon, an injury, or an escalation while you are still on the line, upgrade the priority immediately and advise all responding units over the air before you end the call.
</Warning>

## Unit Status

Units are expected to advise dispatch on every status change. You are responsible for tracking every unit on your channel — if a unit misses a required check-in, you contact them. Do not wait for them to come back on their own.

| Status | Meaning |
| ------ | ------- |
| Available | In service and clear for a new assignment |
| En route | Assigned to a call and travelling to it |
| On scene | Arrived at the call location |
| Busy | In service but committed to a task, not available for new assignment |
| Out of service | Off the road and not available |

## Radio Discipline

The channel belongs to everyone on it. Every time you transmit unnecessarily or at length, you are blocking someone else. Discipline on the air is not a style preference — it is a safety requirement.

<AccordionGroup>

<Accordion title="Listen before transmitting" icon="ear-listen">
Before you key up, confirm the channel is clear. Transmitting over another unit buries their message. If the buried message is a call for help or an emergency traffic declaration, the consequences can be severe. Pause, listen, then transmit.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Keep transmissions short" icon="clock">
Say what needs to be said and release the key. If you need to relay multiple pieces of information, break them into short sequential transmissions rather than holding the channel for an extended block. Every second the channel is occupied is a second no one else can use it.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Use phonetics for anything spelled" icon="radio">
Names, vehicle registrations, plate numbers, and street names all go out in phonetics. A plate that sounds like "BPD 4-2-7" over a degraded signal is unambiguous in phonetics and ambiguous without them. See the [Phonetic Alphabet](/communication/phonetics) page for the full NATO and APCO reference tables.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Emergency traffic takes the channel" icon="triangle-exclamation">
When a unit calls emergency traffic or 10-33, all other units stop transmitting immediately and hold until dispatch clears the channel. There are no exceptions. Acknowledge the emergency, clear all other traffic, and give the floor to the unit in distress.
</Accordion>

</AccordionGroup>
