Good call handling starts before you ever key up the radio. Every call you receive goes through the same intake sequence, gets assigned the same way, and closes the same way — regardless of how quiet or routine it seems at the time. Consistency in your process is what allows you to manage multiple simultaneous incidents without losing track of any of them. This page walks you through every stage of a call in detail, from the first words out of a caller's mouth to the moment you close the incident in the log.

<Tip>
Read the [Dispatch Overview](/dispatch/overview) before this page if you have not already. The priority levels, unit statuses, and radio discipline standards on that page are assumed knowledge throughout this guide.
</Tip>

## Call Intake

When a call comes in, your first job is information gathering — not broadcasting. Do not key up the radio until you have enough information to give a responding unit what they need to respond correctly.

Collect the following in order:

1. **Caller's name** — establishes accountability and allows a callback
2. **Exact location** — street address, block, intersection, or landmark; confirm it
3. **Nature of the call** — what is happening, in the caller's own words
4. **Injuries** — are there any injured persons; if yes, how many and what type
5. **Weapons** — are any weapons involved or visible

<Tip>
If a caller is distressed and skipping around, work through the list anyway — interrupt politely and redirect. "I need your location first before I can send anyone." Callers often give you the most dramatic detail first and leave out the most operationally important detail until you ask for it specifically.
</Tip>

Do not end the call before you have a location you can broadcast. Everything else can be updated after the unit is en route — location cannot.

## Broadcast Procedure

Once you have sufficient intake information, broadcast the call to the appropriate unit. Follow this order on every transmission — priority, location, nature. A unit needs to know how urgently to travel before they need to know where they are going, and they need to know where they are going before they need to know why.

<Steps>

<Step title="Identify the unit by callsign">
Address the specific unit you are assigning. "Unit 4-12" or "Engine One" — not "any available unit" unless no specific unit is more appropriate. Using a callsign keeps the log clean and puts responsibility on a known unit.
</Step>

<Step title="State the priority">
Give the priority level immediately after the callsign. This tells the unit how to travel before you say anything else. "Unit 4-12, Priority 2 call."
</Step>

<Step title="Give the location">
State the full address or cross-street. Spell the street name in phonetics if there is any chance of misinterpretation. "500 block of Main Street — Mike-Alpha-India-November."
</Step>

<Step title="Describe the nature of the call">
Give the nature and any key detail — injuries, weapons, number of parties. "Domestic disturbance, no weapons reported."
</Step>

<Step title="Wait for acknowledgement">
Hold for the unit's acknowledgement before you move on. If there is no acknowledgement within a reasonable time, re-broadcast to the same unit. If still no response, assign an alternate unit and flag the non-responding unit for a welfare check.
</Step>

</Steps>

Here is what a completed broadcast looks like on the air:

```text
Unit 4-12, Priority 2 call — 500 block of Main Street, domestic disturbance, no weapons reported.
```

```text
Engine One, Priority 1 call — 1200 Baseline Road, structure fire, occupants reported inside.
```

```text
Medic Two, Priority 2 call — intersection of Kendall Drive and University Parkway, motor vehicle collision, injuries reported, no entrapment.
```

## Unit Check-Ins and Missed Status Updates

Every unit on your channel is expected to advise you on every status change. If a unit has not updated you within a reasonable time after their last known status, you initiate contact — you do not wait for them.

- **En route with no on-scene update**: Contact the unit after a travel time that exceeds what is reasonable for the distance. "Unit 4-12, status check."
- **On scene with no update**: If a unit has been on scene for an extended period with no traffic at all, initiate a welfare check. "Unit 4-12, dispatch — can you advise status?"
- **No response to welfare check**: Immediately flag for a supervisor and send a cover unit to the last known location. This is treated as a potential officer safety situation until the unit is accounted for.

<Tip>
Keep a simple running note of when each unit last transmitted. You cannot rely on memory alone when you are managing three or four simultaneous calls.
</Tip>

## Managing Multi-Unit Incidents

When two or more units are assigned to the same scene, your coordination role increases. You are the central point — units on scene talk to each other, but they report status and resource requests through you.

- **Designate a primary unit**: One unit is the lead for the scene. All requests for additional resources from that scene come through the lead unit.
- **Log all units assigned**: Every unit at the same scene is logged against the same incident number. Do not create separate incidents for cover units at the same location.
- **Manage channel traffic**: At a busy scene, direct non-priority traffic to hold. "All units, priority traffic on the air — stand by." Clear the channel for the primary scene before handling routine calls.
- **Track clearance**: When the primary unit clears, confirm all support units are clearing with them or advise them of any change in assignment before they leave.

## Handling Requests

Units will make requests during calls. Handle them promptly and return the result clearly.

<AccordionGroup>

<Accordion title="Plate and vehicle registration — 10-28" icon="car">
The unit will provide a plate number in phonetics. Repeat it back for confirmation, run the query, and return the registered owner's name, vehicle description, and any flags. "Unit 4-12, 10-28 on Hotel-Echo-4-2-7 — registered to Jane Smith, 2021 black Ford F-150, no flags." If the vehicle returns stolen, advise immediately and upgrade the call priority.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Driver licence and operator check — 10-27" icon="id-card">
The unit will provide a name and date of birth. Return the licence status, any restrictions, and any relevant flags. Advise the unit if the licence is suspended, expired, or if the subject has a flag for caution. Keep the return concise and spell any names in phonetics.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Wanted or stolen check — 10-29" icon="triangle-exclamation">
Run the subject or vehicle for warrant or stolen status. If the subject or vehicle returns with an active warrant or stolen flag, advise the unit immediately before they approach further if at all possible. "Unit 4-12, 10-29 on that subject returns positive — active warrant, caution advised." A positive 10-29 is treated as a change in call priority and may require additional units.
</Accordion>

</AccordionGroup>

## Call Type Specifics

Different call types require different handling decisions from dispatch. Below are the most common situations you will encounter and what you need to manage actively.

<AccordionGroup>

<Accordion title="Traffic stop — 10-38" icon="octagon-pause">
When a unit initiates a traffic stop, log the location, vehicle description, and plate immediately. The unit should advise you before they approach. If the 10-29 returns a caution flag, advise the unit before they exit their vehicle. Monitor the unit — a traffic stop that goes quiet without an update is treated as a welfare concern. Standard cover unit threshold is one additional unit for a routine stop; more if the vehicle returns with flags or the unit requests cover.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Pursuit — 10-80" icon="car-burst">
A pursuit is a Priority 1 event. Log the originating unit, direction of travel, and vehicle description from the first transmission. Broadcast the 10-80 on the channel immediately so all units are aware. Relay direction and location updates as the pursuing unit calls them. Notify a supervisor as soon as a pursuit is initiated — supervisor authorization may be required to continue depending on your service's policy. Start additional units to anticipate the pursuit's likely termination point. When the pursuit terminates, broadcast the outcome and status of all involved units.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Officer in distress — 10-99" icon="siren">
A 10-99 clears the channel immediately. Broadcast the unit's last known location to all available units, start every available unit toward that location, and notify a supervisor simultaneously. Do not attempt to gather further information before broadcasting — broadcast first, then gather. If the unit cannot respond, use their last known location, last known call assignment, and any background noise you can identify to direct responding units. This is the highest-priority event you will handle.
</Accordion>

</AccordionGroup>

## Escalation — When to Start Additional Resources

You are responsible for recognizing when a call has grown beyond what the assigned resources can handle and starting the response upgrade without waiting to be asked.

Start additional units or services immediately when any of the following occur:

- A unit requests backup or cover
- A weapon is reported at a previously routine call
- An injury is reported at a call not initially reported as medical
- A traffic stop escalates to a pursuit
- A fire call reports active flames, occupants inside, or entrapment
- A unit fails to respond to a welfare check

<Tip>
When in doubt, start the resource and cancel if not needed. Cancelling a unit that is not needed is a minor inconvenience. Failing to start a unit that was needed is a serious failure. The cost of early escalation is always lower than the cost of late escalation.
</Tip>

When escalating to fire or EMS, provide the following to the receiving agency's dispatcher: priority, location, nature of the incident, number of patients or victims if known, and any hazard information. Then log the escalation in the incident record.
